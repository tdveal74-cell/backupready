How to Size a Backup Generator for Your Home (Without Guessing)

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read · Independent buying guide

Sizing is where most people either overspend by thousands on a unit they don't need, or undersize and end up with a generator that trips the first time two appliances kick on at once. The fix isn't complicated — it just takes five minutes with your home's actual loads instead of a guess based on square footage. Here's the method electricians use, simplified.

Running watts vs. starting watts

Every generator spec sheet lists two numbers, and confusing them is the single most common sizing mistake:

  • Running (rated) watts — what the generator supplies continuously, and what an appliance draws once it's already on.
  • Starting (surge) watts — the short burst of extra power a motor-driven appliance needs for a second or two when it first switches on. Often 2–3x the running wattage for compressors, pumps, and motors.

A generator must be sized for the highest combined starting load on top of everything already running — not just the sum of running watts.

The load worksheet

List every circuit or appliance you want backed up, with its running watts and starting watts. Add up all running watts first. Then find the single largest starting-watt surge on your list and add only that one on top.

Typical sizing by home type

  • Essentials only: 3,000–5,000W portable generator.
  • Essentials + window AC or well pump: 5,000–7,500W portable, or 10–12kW standby.
  • Small-to-mid home without central AC: 10–14kW standby typically covers the full panel.
  • Mid-to-large home with central AC: 16–22kW standby is the common range.
  • Large home, multiple AC zones: 22–26kW+, sized by an electrician.

Sizing mistakes to avoid

  • Sizing off square footage instead of actual loads.
  • Ignoring starting watts entirely.
  • Forgetting seasonal loads.
  • Assuming "whole house" means every circuit at once.
  • Skipping a professional load calculation above portable size.

FAQ

What size generator do I need to run my whole house?
Most homes need 10kW–22kW. Homes without central AC often need only 10–14kW; larger homes with central AC and an electric range typically need 18–22kW or more.
What is the difference between running watts and starting watts?
Running watts are the continuous draw once an appliance is on. Starting watts are the brief surge, often 2–3x higher, needed to switch on.
Is it better to oversize a generator?
A modest 10–20% safety margin is worth it. Significantly oversizing wastes money and can mean running inefficiently.
