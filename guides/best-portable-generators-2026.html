Best Portable Generators for Home Backup Power (2026 Buyer's Picks)

Picking a specific model is the step most buyers get stuck on after reading a generic buying guide. Here's how to narrow the field fast, organized by what actually matters: how much power you need, how much you want to spend, and how much noise you can tolerate.

How to Use This Page

Instead of a single "best overall" pick that ignores your situation, use the category that matches your main constraint. Cross-check the wattage number against our generator sizing guide before you commit to a model — buying based on price alone is the most common mistake we see.

Best for Most Homes: Mid-Size Dual Fuel (7,500–10,000 Running Watts)

This is the sweet spot for a typical home backup setup: enough power to run a refrigerator, well pump, furnace blower, and lighting circuits at once, without the cost and footprint of a full standby unit. Look for:

  • Dual fuel (gasoline + propane) capability so you're not dependent on one fuel source during a regional shortage
  • Electric start — pull-start on a generator this size is miserable in cold weather
  • A transfer-switch-ready outlet (L14-30 or similar) rather than only standard household outlets

Expect to pay $700–$1,100 for a reputable model in this class. This is also the size range where fuel type decisions matter most, since you'll likely run it for extended stretches during a multi-day outage.

Best Budget Option: Small Gasoline Inverter (2,000–3,500 Watts)

If your real need is keeping a fridge, some lights, and phone chargers running — not a furnace or well pump — a small inverter generator is cheaper, lighter, and dramatically quieter than a mid-size open-frame unit. Typical price range: $350–$600. The tradeoff is real: this size cannot start most well pumps or central air, so confirm your must-run list against the wattage first using our sizing guide.

Best for Noise-Sensitive Neighborhoods: Inverter Generators Under 58 dB

Open-frame generators in the 7,500+ watt range typically run 70–76 dB at 23 feet — loud enough to draw noise complaints in suburban neighborhoods or HOA communities. If you need meaningful wattage but have close neighbors, look specifically for inverter models rated under 58 dB, or for "dual inverter" systems that let you pair two smaller quiet units in parallel for more power without the noise penalty of a single large unit.

Best for Extended Multi-Day Outages: Large-Tank or Propane-Fed Units

If your region sees multi-day outages (hurricanes, ice storms, wildfire-related grid shutoffs), prioritize run time over peak wattage. Look for a built-in fuel tank rated for 10+ hours at 50% load, or a model with an easy propane conversion so you can run off 100 lb or larger propane tanks instead of making repeated gas station runs. See our runtime and fuel consumption guide for how to estimate total fuel needs for a 3–7 day outage.

What to Check Before You Buy, Regardless of Model

  • Running watts, not just "peak" or "starting" watts — the sticker number is almost always the bigger, less meaningful figure
  • CARB compliance if you live in California or a state that follows CARB emissions rules — non-compliant units can't legally be sold or sometimes even operated there
  • Outlet configuration — confirm it has (or can be fitted with) the right outlet for your transfer switch or interlock setup
  • Warranty length — 3 years is common for mid-range units; anything under 2 years on a generator above $500 is a yellow flag

FAQ

Do I need a specific brand to get parts and service later?

Not strictly, but sticking to brands with a wide dealer/service network (rather than a obscure import-only brand) makes it much easier to get warranty service or parts in year 3–5, which is when small-engine maintenance issues tend to show up. See our maintenance schedule guide for what ongoing care looks like.

Should I buy a slightly bigger generator than I think I need?

Generally yes, within reason. A generator running near its maximum continuous load constantly wears faster and has less headroom if you add an appliance later. Sizing one notch above your calculated minimum is a common and reasonable buffer — see the sizing guide for the math.

Is a more expensive generator always the better buy?

No. Price differences between models in the same wattage class are often driven by noise level, run time, and brand support network rather than raw capability. Match the category above to your actual constraint (budget, noise, run time) rather than assuming higher price means better fit.
