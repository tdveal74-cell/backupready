Gas vs Propane vs Dual Fuel Generators: Which Fuel Type to Buy

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read · Independent buying guide

Every generator runs on gasoline, propane, diesel, or natural gas — and a growing number of portables run on more than one. The fuel decision matters more than most buyers expect: it affects how long your unit can run unattended, how long fuel can sit in storage before an outage, and what it costs to run for a multi-day event. Here's how the three common choices for home backup compare.

Gasoline: cheapest to buy, worst to store

Most portable generators ship gasoline-only because it's the cheapest option up front and gas stations are everywhere. The catch is shelf life: untreated gasoline starts degrading in as little as 30 days and can gum up a carburetor within a season. If you go this route, budget for a fuel stabilizer added at every fill-up, and rotate stored fuel every few months. Gasoline also means a generator-sized fuel plan during a real regional outage — gas stations without power can't pump it either, so your stored supply is all you get until roads clear.

Propane: stores indefinitely, lower output

Propane (LPG) doesn't degrade in storage — a sealed tank is good for 20-plus years, which makes it the better choice for a generator that might sit unused for a year between outages. Standby whole-house units commonly run on propane or natural gas for exactly this reason. The trade-off is roughly 10-15% less power output per gallon-equivalent compared to gasoline, and you need tank capacity on-site: a 20 lb grill tank runs a portable for several hours, while multi-day backup needs 100 lb or larger tanks delivered or exchanged.

Natural gas: unlimited runtime, requires a hookup

If your home already has a natural gas line, a standby generator plumbed into it never runs out of fuel — no tank to refill, no gas station runs. This is the default fuel for most installed whole-house standby systems. The limitation is availability: it only works where gas service already reaches the house, and during a severe regional event gas supply can itself be interrupted, though this is far less common than fuel-delivery disruption.

Dual fuel: the flexible middle ground

Dual fuel portables run on either gasoline or propane, switchable with a selector or included conversion kit. This solves the core problem with either single fuel: keep a tank of propane on hand for guaranteed-ready backup, and fall back to gasoline if propane runs low on a multi-day outage (or vice versa). Dual fuel units typically cost $50-150 more than an equivalent gas-only model, which is modest insurance against being caught with the wrong fuel on hand.

Which should you choose?

  • Grid goes down a few times a year, you're home to manage it: gasoline portable is fine — just commit to stabilizer and rotation.
  • Generator might sit unused for a year, or you want zero maintenance fuel: propane, either portable or standby.
  • Installing a permanent standby unit and have gas service: natural gas removes fuel logistics entirely.
  • Want the safety net without committing to one fuel: dual fuel portable.

FAQ

Can I run a dual fuel generator on propane only, forever? Yes — many owners do, purely for the shelf-stable storage, and only touch the gasoline mode as backup-to-the-backup.

Does propane damage a generator's engine over time? No. Propane burns cleaner than gasoline, which can mean less carbon buildup, though combustion temperatures differ slightly — use the manufacturer's recommended propane jetting or factory dual-fuel kit rather than an aftermarket conversion.

How much propane does a portable generator use? Roughly 2-3 lbs per hour at half load for a 5,000-7,000 watt unit — a standard 20 lb tank lasts about 7-10 hours at moderate load.
