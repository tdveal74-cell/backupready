Transfer Switch vs. Interlock Kit vs. Extension Cords: Safe Generator Hookup Options

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read · Independent safety & buying guide

Buying the right generator (see our buying guide) is only half the problem. How you connect it to your house determines whether you are safe, legal, and able to power what you need.

The backfeeding risk (read this first)

Every year, people are killed or seriously injured because someone connected a generator directly to their home's wiring — plugging it into a wall outlet, called "backfeeding." This is illegal in every U.S. jurisdiction. It can energize the utility line outside your house, electrocuting repair crews who assume the line is dead, and it can destroy your generator and appliances when grid power returns.

Manual transfer switch

A dedicated panel installed next to your main electrical panel with its own breakers for the circuits you want backed up. Plug the generator into an exterior inlet, flip to "generator," and only those circuits draw power, fully isolated from the utility line.

  • Cost: typically $400–$900 installed.
  • Pros: clean circuit selection, no overload risk, works with any portable generator.
  • Cons: higher cost; limited to circuits wired into the switch (typically 6–10).

Interlock kit

A mechanical piece added to your existing main breaker panel that physically blocks the main utility breaker and a generator breaker from both being on at once. Flip off the main, flip on the generator breaker, and your whole panel can draw generator power, one circuit at a time.

  • Cost: typically $150–$400 installed.
  • Pros: cheaper; access to your whole panel instead of a fixed subset.
  • Cons: requires manually managing breakers; not every panel has a listed kit.

Heavy-duty extension cords

For smaller needs, running heavy-duty, outdoor-rated cords directly from the generator to specific appliances is legal and safe, as long as nothing connects to the house's permanent wiring. Lowest cost, lowest commitment.

  • Cost: $20–$60 per cord; no installation needed.
  • Cons: only powers what you can reach with a cord; no furnace or hardwired equipment.

Which one should you get?

SituationBest option
Want the same circuits always ready, minimal thinkingTransfer switch
Want more flexibility for less money, okay managing breakersInterlock kit
Just need a fridge and a few lamps during short outagesHeavy-duty extension cords
Already decided on a standby/whole-house unitNeither — see our portable vs. standby guide

Whichever panel-connected option you choose, this is not a DIY wiring job — have a licensed electrician install it and get it inspected where required.
